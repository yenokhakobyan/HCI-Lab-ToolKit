What is a Kilowatt-Hour (kWh)?

When you pay your electricity bill, you're paying for kilowatt-hours (kWh) — the standard unit of electrical energy consumption. Think of it as a measure of how much electricity you've used over time.

One kWh represents using 1,000 watts of power for one hour. For example, running ten 100-watt light bulbs for one hour uses exactly 1 kWh.

Appliance Power Ratings

Every electrical appliance has a wattage rating that tells you how much power it draws when running:

  • Laptop Computer: 50 watts — low power device
  • Air Conditioner: 1,500 watts — significant power draw
  • Electric Heater: 2,000 watts — highest home power draw
kWh = Watts × Hours ÷ 1,000
Example: 1,500W AC × 4 hours ÷ 1,000 = 6 kWh

How Solar Panels Are Rated

Solar panels are rated by the maximum power they can produce under ideal sunlight conditions, measured in watts (W). A typical residential solar panel today produces between 300 and 400 watts.

Panel Categories

Manufacturers offer different panel tiers to match various budgets and space constraints:

  • Budget Panels (300W): Lower cost, but you need more panels to reach the same output
  • Standard Panels (350W): The most popular choice, balancing cost and performance
  • Premium Panels (400W): Maximum power per panel, ideal when roof space is limited

Calculating System Size

Your total solar system size equals the wattage of each panel multiplied by the number of panels. We express larger systems in kilowatts (kW).

System kW = Panel Watts × Number of Panels ÷ 1,000
Example: 350W × 10 panels ÷ 1,000 = 3.5 kW system

Understanding Solar Installation Costs

A residential solar installation is a significant investment, but the federal government provides a powerful incentive: the Investment Tax Credit (ITC), which allows you to deduct 30% of your solar installation cost from your federal taxes.

System Prices (Before Tax Credit)

  • 4 kW System: $12,000 — suitable for small homes
  • 6 kW System: $18,000 — covers most average household needs
  • 8 kW System: $24,000 — for larger homes with high energy usage

How the Tax Credit Works

The 30% federal tax credit directly reduces what you owe in taxes. For example, if your system costs $18,000, you can claim $5,400 as a tax credit, making your net cost just $12,600.

Net Cost = Price − (Price × 30%)
Example: $18,000 − $5,400 = $12,600 net cost

How Long Until Solar Pays for Itself?

The payback period is the number of years it takes for cumulative energy savings to equal your initial investment. After this point, your solar system generates pure savings.

Calculating Annual Savings

Your annual electricity savings depend on how much energy your solar system produces and your local utility rate:

  • High-rate area ($0.25/kWh): 8,000 kWh/year saves $2,000/year
  • Mid-rate area ($0.15/kWh): 8,000 kWh/year saves $1,200/year
  • Low-rate area ($0.10/kWh): 8,000 kWh/year saves $800/year
Payback Years = Net System Cost ÷ Annual Savings
Example: $12,600 ÷ $2,000/yr = 6.3 years

How Much Energy Will Your System Produce?

The amount of electricity your solar panels generate each day depends on two key factors: your system size and your location's peak sun hours.

Understanding Peak Sun Hours

Peak sun hours represent the equivalent number of hours when sunlight intensity averages 1,000 watts per square meter. This isn't the same as daylight hours — it's a measure of usable solar energy.

Peak Sun Hours by Location

  • Phoenix, AZ: 6.5 hours — desert climate, excellent solar
  • Denver, CO: 5.5 hours — high altitude, good solar
  • Chicago, IL: 4.0 hours — midwest climate, moderate solar
Daily Output = System kW × Peak Sun Hours
Example: 6 kW × 5.5 hours = 33 kWh per day

How Your Electricity Bill Is Calculated

Your monthly electricity bill is determined by multiplying the total energy you consume (in kWh) by the utility rate — the price your electricity provider charges per kilowatt-hour.

Utility Rates by Region

Electricity prices vary significantly across the United States depending on local energy sources and demand:

  • California: $0.25/kWh — among the highest rates nationwide
  • Texas: $0.12/kWh — lower rates due to deregulated market
  • New York: $0.20/kWh — high demand, moderate rates

Calculating Your Monthly Bill

To find your monthly cost, multiply your daily energy usage by 30 days, then by your utility rate.

Monthly Bill = Daily kWh × 30 × Rate per kWh
Example: 30 kWh/day × 30 days × $0.12 = $108/month

Estimating Annual Solar Energy Production

While daily output fluctuates with weather and seasons, solar professionals estimate annual production by multiplying daily output by 365 days and applying a performance factor that accounts for real-world losses.

Performance Factors

Real-world conditions reduce output below the theoretical maximum. Common performance factors:

  • Optimal Tilt & Clean Panels: 85% performance factor
  • Flat Roof / Moderate Shading: 75% performance factor
  • Heavy Shading / Old Panels: 65% performance factor
Annual kWh = Daily kWh × 365 × Performance Factor
Example: 30 kWh/day × 365 × 0.85 = 9,308 kWh/year

Home Battery Storage Systems

Solar panels only generate electricity during daylight. A battery storage system stores excess energy for use at night or during power outages.

Understanding Battery Capacity

Batteries are rated by total capacity, but you can only use a portion safely. The usable capacity depends on the battery chemistry:

  • Lithium-Ion (Li-Ion): 80% usable capacity, 10-year lifespan
  • Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP): 90% usable capacity, 15-year lifespan
  • Lead-Acid: 50% usable capacity, 4-year lifespan

Products on the Market

  • Tesla Powerwall: 13.5 kWh rated, Li-Ion, $9,000
  • LG Chem RESU: 10 kWh rated, Li-Ion, $6,500
  • Enphase IQ: 5 kWh rated, LFP, $4,500
Usable kWh = Rated Capacity × Usable %
Example: 10 kWh × 80% = 8 kWh usable

Grid-Tied vs Off-Grid Solar Systems

When designing a solar system, homeowners must choose between staying connected to the grid or going completely off-grid. Each approach has different costs and trade-offs.

Grid-Tied Systems

A grid-tied system uses the utility grid as backup. Excess solar power is exported to the grid for bill credits (net metering). No battery is required.

  • Net cost after tax credit: $12,600
  • Monthly savings: $150 (electricity offset)
  • Reliability: Grid serves as backup; power out during grid outages

Off-Grid Systems

Off-grid systems need batteries to store all energy. This significantly increases cost but provides complete energy independence.

  • Net cost after tax credit: $29,400 (panels + battery bank)
  • Monthly savings: $200 (no utility bill at all)
  • Reliability: Fully independent; works during outages

10-Year Financial Comparison

Grid-Tied: $150/mo × 120 months = $18,000 savings − $12,600 cost = net +$5,400

Off-Grid: $200/mo × 120 months = $24,000 savings − $29,400 cost = net −$5,400

Key Insight: Off-grid costs more upfront and results in a net loss over 10 years. Grid-tied is cheaper and produces a net gain, but lacks outage protection.

Choosing the Right Solar Inverter

An inverter converts the DC electricity from solar panels into AC electricity your home can use. The inverter must be properly sized and matched to your system.

Inverter Types

  • String Inverter: One unit handles all panels. Capacity: 3–10 kW, Efficiency: 96%, Cost: $1,200, Warranty: 10 years
  • Microinverters: One per panel. Capacity: per panel, Efficiency: 97%, Cost: $2,400 (set of 12), Warranty: 25 years
  • Power Optimizer + String: Optimizers on each panel plus central inverter. Capacity: 3–10 kW, Efficiency: 98%, Cost: $2,000, Warranty: 12 years

When Shading Matters

With string inverters, one shaded panel reduces the output of all panels. Microinverters and optimizers allow each panel to operate independently, so shading on one panel doesn't affect others.

Key Insight: If your roof has partial shading, microinverters or optimizers protect overall system performance. Unshaded roofs can save money with a string inverter.
